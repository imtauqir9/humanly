Gather Before a word is written

  1. 0

    Your brief

    A topic, the angle you want, and three to five first-person lines: what you have seen, built or believe. The take is written into the article in your words, and the audit later checks it survived. It is the single biggest reason an article stops reading as generic.

    You provideTopic, intent, your take. Optionally your own research notes, and whether you want a LinkedIn post, video script or share card.
  2. 1

    Search research

    Your intent becomes search keywords, and the top live Google results are read for what is ranking, who it is written for, the subtopics they cover and the questions they leave open.

    • A voice profile is built from your sample articles, and every step that touches prose is held to it.
    Why it mattersThe article is built around the gaps in what already ranks, not around a summary of it.
  3. 1.4

    Your research notes New

    If you have already done the reading, hand it over. Your notes become the first fact pack, built from the notes alone, with nothing added from the model's memory. Each figure keeps the URL your notes give it and the sentence they use.

    • Anything your notes mark as unverified, secondhand or "to verify" is not treated as a fact. It goes to a list of gaps the writer must check, not state.
    • Your notes' angle becomes the article's angle.
    Why it mattersYour own research is used as evidence, with its caveats kept intact.
  4. 1.5

    The fact pack

    The pipeline opens the primary pages itself (vendor pricing, documentation, exam guides, first-party reports) and pins every specific figure to a URL and a verbatim quote. These facts are merged in after yours.

    • The writer may state no price, date, version or statistic that is not in the pack.
    • A pack value can never be widened into a range.
    • If nothing could be verified, the article states no figures at all and says plainly where a number would go.
    The ruleEvery number in the article can be traced to the page it came from. The pack is saved alongside the article.

Plan Shape before prose

  1. 2

    Title

    Picks the strongest angle and the keyword it should rank for.

    OutputA refined title and a target keyword.
  2. 3

    Key takeaways

    Works out what the article has to cover to beat what currently ranks.

    OutputThe must-cover list.
  3. 4

    Outline, in three levels

    Every article teaches in layers so a newcomer and an expert both get something:

    • The simple version, with an analogy, an example, terms defined and a diagram.
    • How it actually works.
    • Where it gets hard.
    Why it mattersThis outline is the contract. The auditor later holds the draft to it.

Write One draft, grounded

  1. 5

    The draft

    A full article written from the fact pack, your sample articles, your voice profile and your take. Every figure is cited inline to its source.

    LengthLong-form by default, 2,500 to 3,500 words; shorter 1,000 and 2,000-word versions are available.

Edit Measured, not guessed

  1. 6

    Humanize, in three passes

    • Pass 1 removes AI patterns against a 24-item checklist.
    • Pass 2 the model audits its own draft.
    • Pass 3 a scanner counts the tells that actually survived (stock vocabulary, title-cased headings, em dashes, sentences of uniform length) and the model repairs only the phrases it names. If the count goes up, pass 2 is kept.
    Why it mattersThe last pass is checked by counting, so it cannot quietly make things worse.
  2. 6.2

    Plain-language check

    Measures the simple version's sentences: an average of 17 words or fewer, none over 30. If the section is too dense, only that section is rewritten.

    Why it mattersThe opening stays readable for someone new to the subject.
  3. 6.4

    The answer block

    A 40 to 60 word answer placed under the headline, written to make sense when quoted on its own.

    Why it mattersIt is the part search and AI answer engines lift and quote.

Verify The argument

  1. 6.5

    A second model audits, the writer argues back

    The finished draft goes to a model from a different vendor, whose only job is to find what is wrong with it. It checks the draft against the brief for factual support, fidelity to the outline, voice and coverage, and every finding is anchored to a quoted passage.

    Audit
    A second vendor scores the draft and returns findings tied to quotes.
    Rebut
    The writer accepts or disputes each one. Silence counts as accepting.
    Judge
    A third model rules on whatever is still contested.
    Fix
    Only accepted or upheld findings are applied.
    Then a fresh audit, for up to two rounds.
    Why three modelsA blind spot shared by the writer and the auditor would never be raised, so the auditor runs on a different vendor first, and the judge on a third where possible. A review report is saved with every article.

Ship Ready to publish

  1. 7

    Search metadata

    SEO title, meta description and URL slug, plus a full HTML head: canonical link, Open Graph and Twitter cards, and Article and FAQ structured data built from the article's own FAQ.

    Why it mattersSearch engines and answer engines get the article's structure, not just its text.
  2. 8

    Images

    Real images found through Google Images or Unsplash and embedded in the Word file. No placeholders.

    OutputImages embedded in the DOCX.
  3. 8.5

    The diagram

    The diagram planned in the outline is drawn by the pipeline itself, as a flow, cycle, layers or comparison, not searched for online.

    OutputSVG inside the web version, PNG in Markdown and Word.
  4. +

    Optional extras

    From the finished, verified article: a LinkedIn post, a video script, a share card, and an MP4 with a voiceover.

    Why it mattersOne piece of research, several formats, all from the same checked facts.
ii.Research to publish

Search before you write, check at the source, search again after

The pipeline is one part of a nine-step loop. Humanly runs steps 4 to 6 in a single command. The steps around them stay with the author, and two of them are search checkpoints.

  1. 1

    Pick the topic

    From the topic radar or a research report: a gap nobody covers well, with a dated trigger.

    By hand
  2. 2

    Search check

    Search volume, what ranks and the AI Overview. The headline becomes the exact query people type.

    Checkpoint
  3. 3

    Research notes

    A URL and a date on every claim, and a [VERIFY] marker on the shaky ones.

    By hand
  4. 4

    Fact packs

    One from your notes, one from the web, merged. Flagged claims go to the gaps.

    Humanly
  5. 5

    Write and verify

    Outline, draft, three humanizing passes, then audit, rebut, judge and fix, for up to two rounds.

    Humanly
  6. 6

    Package

    Article, metadata, FAQ, diagram, LinkedIn post, video script and share card. Carousels are made by hand.

    Humanly
  7. 7

    Hand check

    Every number and quote re-read at its source. If it fails, confirm it or cut it.

    By hand
  8. 8

    Publish in order

    YouTube, then the article, then the post with the link in the first comment, then the newsletter and your own domain.

    By hand
  9. 9

    Measure

    Search Console, LinkedIn and AI citations at day 7 and day 30. The results pick the next topic.

    Checkpoint

Step 9 feeds step 1. Each round of publishing informs the next topic.

v.The house rules

What the pipeline will not do

These are enforced in the pipeline, not left to the model's judgement.

  1. State a figure it cannot source. No price, date, version or statistic outside the fact pack.
  2. Treat a flagged claim as fact. Anything marked unverified is sent back to be checked.
  3. Fill gaps from memory. Where a number is missing, the article says so.
  4. Mark its own homework. The audit runs on a different model from the one that wrote the draft.
  5. Apply a finding nobody agreed on. Only accepted or upheld findings change the text.
  6. Drop your voice. Your take is written in first person, and the audit checks it is still there.
